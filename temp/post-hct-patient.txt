Event-Free Survival Prediction for Post-HCT 
Patients Using Ensemble Methods and Identifying 
Patient Clusters

Abstract— Hematopoietic cell transplantation (HCT) 
remains a curative yet high-risk procedure for patients. 
Predicting post-HCT survival is crucial for treatment strategies. 
Traditional clinical scoring systems often have limited 
predictive power due to complexity of patient characteristics. 
This study aims to develop and evaluate a machine learning
based predictive model for binary classification of event-free 
survival in post-HCT patients, while emphasizing high recall 
score. The dataset was obtained from Kaggle, and went through 
data preprocessing, followed by six machine learning models. 
Light Gradient Boosting Machine was the best model achieving 
0.94 recall, 0.80 precision and an AUC of 0.74 after 
hyperparameter optimization. Feature selection and feature 
importance was carried out to reduce as well as increase 
interpretability of the model. Clustering was performed to find 
and group patients based on similar health records. Despite 
considerable noise in the data, most algorithms consistently 
captured patterns related to HLA attributes. The findings 
confirm that machine learning can be used to effectively aid in 
survival prediction of post-HCT patients. Prioritizing recall 
ensured clinical reliability by minimizing false negatives.

I. INTRODUCTION 
Allogenic haematopoietic cell transplantation (allo-HCT) 
is a fundamental and potentially curative treatment for patients 
suffering from a variety of haematological malignancies and 
bone marrow-related disorders [1] [2] [3]. The increased 
utilisation of allo-HCT is supported by factors such as 
enhanced alternative donor availability [3]. Despite these 
advancements, allo-HCT procedures are associated with 
considerably high rates of complications [3]. Major 
complications are recognized as primary causes of death in 
allo-HCT patients followed by cardiovascular disease. 
Accurately predicting survival outcomes for post-HCT 
patients is a challenge of high clinical importance. An accurate 
prognosis is essential for timely decision-making, including 
determining transplant eligibility, selecting appropriate 
donors, and planning preparatory actions and treatment 
methods [1] [4] [3]. The success rate of HCT is significantly 
dependent on prior examination, data collection, and analysis. 
By utilising machine learning (ML), one can establish a 
reliable prior prognosis, by which healthcare professionals can 
take necessary actions based on predicted outcomes [1]. 
Traditional systems developed for post-HCT survival 
prediction have often exhibited suboptimal prediction power. 
Existing scoring systems, such as the HCT-specific 
Comorbidity Index (HCT-CI), the Disease Risk Index (DRI), 
and the European Group for Blood and Marrow 
Transplantation (EBMT) risk score, typically rely on 
conventional parametric statistical methodologies. The Area 
Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC
ROC) for such models lies between 0.6 and 0.7 [3]. 
The challenge arises due to the nature of data generated in 
healthcare characterised by high volume and high complexity, 
particularly for HCT as outcomes are dependent on multiple 
variables. Survivability varies based on numerous factors [5] 
[3]. Traditional statistical methods are often unsuitable for 
analysis considering the complex interactions between the 
variables [3]. There is also a need for the development of novel 
models that can more accurately predict long-term survival 
and overall survival (OS) in HCT recipients [2]. 
ML is a subset of Artificial Intelligence (AI), and it offers 
a powerful solution to overcome the limitations of traditional 
computing methods by employing advanced analytical tools. 
ML algorithms are designed to analyse and model vast 
quantities of complicated data, identifying hidden patterns that 
conventional approaches cannot capture. The application of 
ML is instrumental in enhancing decision-making [6]. ML 
models can concurrently apply multiple clinical and 
laboratory variables, thereby reducing potential biases 
inherent in models relying on simpler analyses [3]. The goal 
of ML is to find patterns within the data that can generate 
models capable of making predictions on new unforeseen data 
[5]. 
The success of an ML model depends on the quality of its 
input data [5] [7]. Raw data is complex, noisy, and may 
contain missing or inconsistent values. Therefore, data 
preprocessing is essential for cleaning, integrating, 
transforming, and reducing data [5] [7]. Filling missing values 
and converting input data into numerical form is essential 
before training ML models [1]. Clustering is used to identify 
unrecognized clusters or subgroups [6]. This approach is 
essential for patient subtyping based on similarities [8]. 
Feature selection is used to reduce the number of features 
while retaining maximum predictive information. It is also 
called as dimensionality reduction [6]. One purpose of 
dimensionality reduction is to ensure optimal prediction with 
minimal time and computation [1]. 
The use of advanced ML algorithms for classification is 
important for prediction of survival outcome. The field 
benefits from algorithms like Logistic Regression (LR), 
Decision Tree (DT), K-Nearest Neighbours (K-NN), and 
powerful ensemble methods like Random Forest (RF) and 
Gradient Boosting Machines (GBM) [5]. Ensemble methods 
combine multiple weaker models to achieve increased 
accuracy and stability, and also help in mitigating overfitting 
[5] [6]. Assessing how well the model has performed is vital,

certain hyperparameters. In the case of classification, metrics 
such as accuracy, AUC, recall (sensitivity), precision and F1

The main aim for this study is to develop a robust ML 
classification model that predicts a binary survival outcome 
(event-free, event) for patients that have undergone HCT with 
considerably high recall thereby minimizing the risk of high
risk cases. Model should be able to support categorical 
features and missing values. Model interpretability should 
also be high enough so that professionals can understand how 
different values for a certain attribute can affect a patient. This 
involves systematically preprocessing the data as, employing 
clustering and dimensionality reduction to find optimum 
features, and testing different advanced ML algorithms to find 
most suitable model. This study uses a retrospective, multi
dimensional clinal dataset that has been synthesised to conceal 
patient information using a real-world dataset from the Centre 
for International Blood and Marrow Transplant Research 
(CIBMTR).  
This model offers an evidence-based method for early risk 
detection in post-HCT care. This approach aims to improve 
predictive performance over traditional tools by harnessing 
advanced ML algorithms. High recall ensures that high-risk 
patients are identified, thereby supporting timely intervention. 
Furthermore, the methodological framework established in 
this research is adaptable and scalable, enabling broader 
applications across different patient populations, and 
advancing the use of ML in clinical survival prediction.  
II. LITERATURE SURVEY 
The primary objective of developing ML models in 
healthcare is to generate insights, and models capable of 
making accurate predictions involving complex clinical data 
[5]. ML is gaining popularity over traditional approaches due 
to its ability to analyse and integrate large amounts of 
complicated healthcare data for prediction and classification 
tasks [6]. 
Data preprocessing is a crucial step, often consuming  50% - 80% of the entire process [7]. For tabular data, effective 
preprocessing typically involves, noise reduction, handling 
missing values, scaling and feature selection. High
dimensional datasets usually  have multicollinearity or 
features that function largely as noise, which can deteriorate a 
model’s performance [5] [7]. There are several methods to 
optimise feature sets. One study utilised chi-square testing to 
reduce 59 initial features down to top 11 to predict survival in 
children undergoing HCT, achieving the same maximum 
prediction accuracy (94.73%) while requiring lower time and 
computation [1]. Wrapper methods such as Forward 
Sequential Selection (FSS) and Backward Feature 
Elimination (BFE) can also be used to ensure predictive 
variables are minimally redundant [4] [6]. Recursive Feature 
Elimination (RFE) is used to rank each variable based on its

They noted that standard scores, National Early Warning

patients with diverse presentations, suggesting that 
incorporating patient subtypes could improve risk assessment 
[8].  
Prioritizing high recall (sensitivity) highlights a specific 
research gap, particularly in dealing with healthcare domain 
as it is associated with rare events. In a medical context like 
HCT, a false negative (FN) would mean predicting that a 
patient will be event-free when, in reality, they suffer the event 
(death, relapse, graft failure). Minimizing FNs is often critical, 
as these misclassifications can severely impact a patient's life 
and clinical treatment pathway [1] [5]. Incorporating model 
interpretability tools like SHapley Additive exPlanations 
(SHAP) is essential, as these scores quantify each feature's 
contribution to individual predictions, helping in understand 
why a survival event was predicted, thus building clinical trust 
[3] [4].

III. DATASET DESCRIPTION 
The dataset was collected by going through a Kaggle 
competition titled, "Equity in Post-HCT Survival Predictions" 
[11]. It consists of synthetically generated, de-identified 
patient records from the CIBMTR registry, albeit preserves 
realistic clinical structure and distribution. It focuses on 
predicting survival outcomes for patients undergoing 
allogenic HCT. The dataset contains 28,000 instances and 59 
variables including demographic, clinical and donor/recipient 
compatibility information. The dataset was de-identified to 
protect patient privacy. The features are mostly categorical 
with mainly two numerical features, one bimodal and other 
multimodal.  
 
The target variable EFS, indicates event-free survival 
status, with 0 representing no event and one representing 
occurrence of an event. The class labels are distributed fairly

presence of many missing values, with two features having

much time a patient might suffer an event, if such an event 
occurs given their medical record. This study does not make

V. PREPROCESSING 
The preprocessing phase was there to ensure data 
consistency, quality and readiness for model training. Based 
on insights from the EDA phase, several steps were taken to 
clean data. Firstly, features that seemed irrelevant were 
removed. Then, due to presence of large number of missing 
values, the approach used was for the numerical features, 
missing values were replaced with -1 value. Whereas, for 
categorical features, missing values were replaced with a 
“Missing” category. This was done so that the ML model 
identifies and acknowledges missing data, and builds a model 
taking that into consideration. As the dataset was from a 
healthcare domain, filling missing values with arbitrary values 
seemed incorrect as that might create unnecessary and 
possibly incorrect relationships. Some features were assigned 
the wrong datatype, which were corrected.  
Feature binning was performed on the two available 
features using equal-width bin technique. A feature was 
binned into five bins, and the other was binned into ten bins 
based on baseline LGBM model performance. The dataset 
which then was totally categorical, was encoded to get 
numerical values, which was necessary in the upcoming steps. 
Few feature selection techniques like Sequential Forward 
Selection (SFS), Sequential Backward Feature (SBS) and 
Recursive Feature Selection (RFE) were employed to identify 
the top ten features. However, none of them matched the 
performance of the full feature set. Therefore, complete set of 
engineered and encoded features was used for classification 
and clustering. 
After each major change to the dataset, a baseline model 
was made using LGBM to check for data quality, and to also 
get a baseline performance statistic. Multiple pre-processed 
dataset versions were then generated for comparative analysis. 
The first dataset retained the original categorical and 
numerical features except the irrelevant features. After that, 
missing values were handled, which then formed the second 
dataset. The third dataset was created by applying feature 
binning on the numerical features, and converting them into 
categorical features. Lastly, the entire dataset was encoded for 
algorithms and techniques that require numerical input to 
perform. The last two datasets produced the same metrics, 
establishing the native support of categorical features by 
LGBM, when categorical features are passed as parameters. 
A. Classification 
In medical prediction tasks, evaluation metrics must be 
selected keeping in mind clinical priorities. In this study, the 
primary metric to be used is recall, as FNs can have severe 
clinical implications. Recall (Sensitivity) measures the 
proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified by the 
model. A high recall value ensures that most patients who are 
actually at risk are detected.  
Recall =

True Positives + False Negatives
Precision is another metric being used in this study. It 
measures the proportion of predicted class that are actually 
belonging to that class. This is useful and should be high as in 
healthcare, low precision leads to unnecessary panic on 
patients and additional strain on clinical resources which can 
otherwise be utilized by someone truly affected. 
Precision = 
True Positives
True Positives + False Positives
F1-score is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, 
balancing both metrics into a single value. It is valuable 
especially when dealing with imbalanced datasets. In medical 
applications, a high f1-score means that a model can detect 
true cases reliably without raising many false alarms. 
F1 Score = 2 x Precision x Recall
Precision + Recall
Along with recall, precision and f1-score, Area Under the 
Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (ROC-AUC) is 
considered as another important metric. It provides a measure 
of the model’s ability to distinguish between the two classes 
across various threshold values. It is useful for comparing 
multiple models’ discriminative capabilities. Finally, accuracy 
is reported for completeness, but it is not the primary metric 
used to evaluate models in this study. 
Accuracy =
True Positives+True Negatives
True Positive + True Negatives+ False Positives+False Negatives
Overall, the evaluation strategy in this study is chosen to 
maximise recall first for class 1, ensuring minimal false 
negatives, with a strong enough precision score for class 0, 
followed by AUC score for assessing discriminative power, 
and lastly accuracy as a general performance indicator.

VII. CLASSIFICATION 
After preparing the data, several models were trained on 
the encoded dataset. The algorithms included LGBM, 
CatBoost, AdB, RF, XGB, and NB. LGBM and CatBoost have 
native support for categorical features, hence categorical 
features were declared to both of them as parameters. Each 
model was trained using same train-test split, and the target 
feature was stratified. All these models were evaluated based 
on the aforementioned metrics. To verify the stability of these 
results, all models underwent 5-fold cross validation, and all 
classification related metrics were recorded. At the end, mean 
of all runs for that metric along with the standard deviation 
were measured to quantify the results. Among the models, 
LGBM and CatBoost performed the strongest but due to high 
time requirement of CatBoost, further optimizations and steps 
proceeded with LGBM. 
Hyperparameter tuning was carried out for LGBM using 
two approaches. First, the classical grid search with cross 
validation was performed to find the best parameters. Second, 
Optuna was used for advanced optimization. The model tuned 
by Optuna achieved the highest performance, improving both 
recall and precision scores with a fairly good ROC-AUC score 
as compared to previous configurations.  
To better interpret the results and the model, as is crucial 
in the field of medicine, feature importance scores were 
calculated for the best performing LGBM variant. The 
associated tree was plotted for doctors and professionals to 
understand how and why a prediction was made. Also, SHAP 
was used to further interpret the findings by plotting a bee 
of the model’s decision process. 
VIII. CLUSTERING 
Clustering was conducted to explore the inherent structure 
of the dataset without relying on the target feature. Two pre
processed datasets were used, one with fully numerically 
encoded values, and the other without any encoding to help in 
visualising. Due to the large size of the dataset, random 
sampling was performed and 25% of the original data was 
used to perform clustering due to time and computational 
constraints. Firstly, pairwise distances were calculated using 
Hamming metric due to presence of categorical features. 
These distances were used in multiple clustering algorithms to 
capture relationships in the data. Five clustering algorithms 
were used, namely, K-Means, K-Modes, AHC, DBSCAN and 
HDBSCAN. 
K-Means was implemented using K-Means++ 
initialization and evaluated on all clustering related metrics to 
find best cluster size. K-Modes is a special case of the K
Means algorithm but for categorical features only, which 
underwent the same process, but using Huang initialization to 
find optimal number of clusters. Next, AHC was used with 
linkage method set as average and clustering metrics were 
used to find best cluster size. Both, DBSCAN and HDBSCAN 
are algorithms that form clusters based on the parameter 
values due to the way they work. So, DBSCAN was iteratively 
tested to find best epsilon and minimum sample parameters on 
the basis of a good silhouette score, and so was HDBSCAN 
with parameters, minimum cluster size and minimum samples 
parameter. 
After finding the optimal number of clusters for each 
algorithm, clusters were formed based on those findings. 
These clusters were then analysed using graphs and the 
unencoded dataset to better understand the different sub
groups formed and what categories they contained. This 
comparative analysis enabled a comprehensive understanding 
of how different clustering techniques captured the hidden 
patterns in the data.  
IX. IMPLEMENTATION DETAILS 
All experiments were implemented using the Python 
programming language (version 3.11.7) in a Jupyter Lab 
environment with Anaconda. The libraries included Pandas, 
NumPy, scikit-learn, XGBoost, LightGBM, CatBoost, 
Matplotlib, Seaborn, dtreeviz, kmodes, Optuna and SHAP. All 
random states were fixed at 42 for reproducibility. A custom 
metric combining precision (class 0), recall (class 1) and ROC
AUC was used for evaluation. 
The experiments were executed on a Windows 11 
operating system with Intel Core i5 12th Gen and 16 GB 
RAM. The entire workflow was implemented and executed 
within a virtual environment. Raw, interim and processed data 
were stored in CSV files with appropriate names. All code, 
data and supporting resources used in this study are openly 
accessible on my GitHub repository [12].

patients. Five clustering algorithms were used which were K
Means, K-Modes, AHC, DBSCAN and HDBSCAN. Target 
feature was removed from the dataset and the above 
algorithms were tested with multiple hyperparameter values. 
Due to presence of categorical features, conventional distance 
metrics were unsuitable, therefore this study employed 
pairwise hamming distance.

XI. CONCLUSION 
This study was conducted to evaluate whether ML could 
enhance survival prediction for patients undergoing allo-HCT, 
particularly emphasizing recall to minimise FNs. The results 
demonstrate that modern ML approaches offer clear 
advantages over traditional techniques when dealing with 
complex, heterogenous clinical data. Among the evaluated 
models, LGBM stood out as the most optimal model, with 
high recall, precision and strong ROC-AUC. This model was 
a resultant of being optimized with Optuna to find best 
hyperparameter values. 
Interpretability analysis, including feature importance, 
decision tree visualisation and SHAP values reinforced the 
clinical relevance of several key predictors. These insights 
support the idea that ML can complement clinical judgement 
by highlighting variables that most strongly associated with 
outcomes. Clustering was used to examine whether the data 
contained meaningful sub-groups. Although the overall 
structure was noisy and weakly defined, multiple algorithms 
consistently differentiated patients based on HLA-related 
characteristics. 
Overall, the study confirms that ML-based survival 
prediction is both feasible and clinically valuable for post
HCT care. By prioritizing recall, the model ensures that high
risk patients are identified early, supporting timely 
interventions. The methodology developed here is scalable, 
transparent and adaptable, offering a strong foundation for 
future work on personalized risk prediction in transplant 
medicine.
